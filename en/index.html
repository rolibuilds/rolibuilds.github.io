Built

From Reporting to Building

I have spent nearly seven years working with financial processes, reports and forecasts. During that time, I saw how easily manual steps and spreadsheets become permanent parts of a process, even when everyone involved knows there should be a better way.

I still like Excel. It helped me automate my first monthly report and showed me how much time a practical solution can give back. But it also made me curious about what comes next, especially when the problem is bigger than a single file.

Later, I discovered Power BI and saw an opportunity to turn that same financial report, spread across more than 20 Excel worksheets, into something more focused, visual and useful for management. It also gave me my first opportunity at OTP Bank to work directly with a managing director and understand what senior management actually needed from the report.

That same curiosity eventually led me to Power Platform. And here I am, building CSAP with Copilot and Claude on my team. I started with only very limited hands-on Power Platform experience, worked through the business and technical decisions, tested what I built and changed direction when the evidence showed that I should.

I am still learning, but I am no longer only describing how financial processes work. I can now start building a better version of them.

  4. Moving beyond the spreadsheet

    From Excel reporting to Power BI

    The VBA solution made the monthly process much faster, but the report still lived in an Excel workbook with more than 20 worksheets. It needed regular maintenance, and I felt we had reached the point where further automation would only improve the existing format rather than move beyond it.

    The VBA solution made the monthly process much faster, but the report still lived in an Excel workbook with more than 20 worksheets. It needed regular maintenance, and I felt we had reached the point where further automation would only improve the existing format rather than move beyond it.

    When I discovered Power BI, I saw an opportunity to rethink the same report.

    Before we started designing the new solution, I worked directly with the managing director to understand what information was actually needed. The aim was to focus on the figures and trends that supported management decisions and remove everything that added little value.

    I initiated the move to Power BI and represented the business side throughout the project. I translated the existing financial logic into requirements, contributed to the dashboard design and tested the new output against the established report. Once the figures and analytical logic were working as intended, I provided business acceptance. The technical development was carried out by an external specialist.

Corporate Services Automation Platform

CSAP flagship

CSAP is my personal portfolio project, built around a realistic business problem: how to manage requests that require both approval and financial control without relying on disconnected emails and spreadsheets.

The solution uses Dataverse to hold the organisational, financial and request data, while Power Automate manages submission, validation and approval logic.

The current implementation includes financial validation, multi-currency handling, budget-capacity checks, approval routing, clarification and exception handling, and a traceable approval history. A separate FX-rate process prepares official MNB exchange-rate data for import, helping keep the rates used by the solution up to date.

I am not trying to imitate a large enterprise platform or add complexity for its own sake. My aim with CSAP is to gain hands-on, practical experience by building a focused MVP that is understandable, testable and useful, with the decisions and trade-offs documented along the way.

Building this as a private individual comes with technical limitations. I treat those as design constraints rather than excuses: simplify where it makes sense, find practical ways to test the important controls and clearly document what could not be validated in the same way as it would be in a full organisational environment.

Two books at a time

I usually have two books on the go: one that teaches me something and one that simply pulls me into a good story.

Recently, those were The Richest Man in Babylon and Stephen King’s 11.22.63.

11.22.63 took its time getting started, but once it drew me in, it really did. I follow American politics daily, and I like stories that weave real events deeply into fiction, such as Tamás Frei’s Kobalt.

What stayed with me most from The Richest Man in Babylon was the story of Dabasir, the camel trader: someone who could only regain freedom and self-respect by stopping his escape from responsibility.

My takeaway wasn’t simply that hard work leads to success. It was that determination becomes meaningful when it’s followed by responsibility, useful work and the willingness to develop real expertise.

That idea connects naturally with how I approach automation and AI. Not as a shortcut, but as a field where consistent effort, practical work and growing competence can create genuine value.

More than a tool

AI first caught my attention because I wanted to understand how it could support my work. Somewhere along the way, it also became something I genuinely enjoy using and creating with.

What I don’t need is an AI assistant that agrees with everything I say. I personalise every tool I use to challenge me, question weak assumptions and tell me when there may be a better approach.

I return the favour. If an answer sounds convincing but doesn’t survive testing, I change it. If a suggestion adds unnecessary complexity, I reject it. If the first idea is weak, we keep working until it becomes something I’m prepared to stand behind.

That’s what makes AI more than a productivity tool for me. It gives me a practical way to learn, create and explore ideas that I couldn’t have implemented on my own before.
